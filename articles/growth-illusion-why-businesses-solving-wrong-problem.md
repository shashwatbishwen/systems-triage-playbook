# The Growth Illusion: Why Businesses Are Solving the Wrong Problem

> **Category:** Growth & Customer Strategy | **Author:** [Dr. Shashwat Bishwen](https://shashwat.si) | **Canonical Link:** [https://shashwat.si/articles/growth-illusion-why-businesses-solving-wrong-problem/](https://shashwat.si/articles/growth-illusion-why-businesses-solving-wrong-problem/)

## Executive Summary
Businesses have become very good at broadcasting what they do and measuring how many people see them. But attention is only an opportunity. A customer does not choose a business merely because they have seen it; they choose when enough things come together for that choice to make sense.

## Deep Dive

During an evening rendezvous, [Jeet Shekhawat](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jeet10xofficial/), who has seen this card countless times as we meet people to understand the problem statement, suggested that I should perhaps change the words "Consulting Criminal" the next time I print my cards.

I asked him why.

He felt the words might need a little explanation. Perhaps something more conventional would make it easier for people to understand what I do.

He had a point, at least from the perspective of conventional business communication.

Look at most visiting cards. They are miniature company profiles. A logo, a designation, a list of services, certifications, credentials, websites, phone numbers and sometimes a QR code leading to yet another collection of information. We seem to believe that if we put enough information in front of someone, we increase the likelihood of being remembered.

I have never quite agreed with that assumption.

A visiting card is not a brochure. It is not supposed to close a deal, explain a business or establish someone's entire professional credibility. Its job is much simpler: leave a memory.

My card carries my name, an unusual designation and the words "Leveraging Logic". The reverse says just two words: "Let's talk!" followed by my contact details.

I don't expect the card to answer every question. I want it to create one.

There is a difference between giving someone all the information and giving them a reason to remember you. Sometimes, the effort to explain everything leaves nothing particularly memorable behind.

That led me to ask Jeet a broader question. What, in his view, is the biggest problem businesses are trying to solve today?

"Visibility," he said. Businesses want to be seen. They want more people to know they exist and, ultimately, bring customers to their doorstep.

It is probably the answer most marketing teams would give.

I disagreed.

Not because visibility is unimportant, but because I think we have become so accustomed to discussing it that we have stopped examining what must happen before and after it.

A business can be visible everywhere and still be invisible to the customer who matters.

That sounds contradictory, but think about it. How many businesses have websites, social media accounts, advertising campaigns and a steady stream of content, yet struggle to attract the right customers? How many receive enquiries that go nowhere? How many have excellent products or services but find themselves repeatedly explaining why anyone should care?

They are not necessarily suffering from a lack of visibility.

They may be solving the wrong problem.

### The customer does not begin where your marketing begins

Businesses tend to organise themselves around what they sell. Their websites describe their capabilities. Their presentations explain their services. Their advertisements communicate their differentiators. Their sales teams talk about features, experience and competitive advantages.

There is nothing inherently wrong with this. A business must communicate what it offers.

But the customer is not beginning from the same place.

A customer begins with a need, a frustration, a constraint, an ambition or sometimes a vague feeling that something could be better. They may not know the name of the solution. They may not know which category of provider to approach. They may not even have fully understood the problem yet.

A hospital struggling with disconnected systems may think it needs another software application. A company experiencing operational inefficiency may believe it needs more people. A business whose technology platform is slowing down may assume it needs a complete replacement.

The customer has a problem, but their understanding of the problem may be incomplete.

Now imagine a business that has the expertise to help. It has the right people, a credible track record and a solution that could make a meaningful difference.

But its communication says, "We provide enterprise technology consulting."

The customer does not search for that sentence. The customer searches for a way out of the problem they are experiencing.

The two may never meet.

This is the first gap I wanted to understand more clearly: the distance between a business having something valuable to offer and a customer being able to find it when it becomes relevant.

I call this **Discoverability**.

It is not simply about ranking on Google, appearing in advertisements or being present on every possible platform. It is about being findable in the context of a need. Sometimes the business must be discoverable through the problem itself, not merely through the name of the solution.

An observation made by [Chandan Mendiratta](https://www.linkedin.com/in/cmendi/) in a public conversation about consumer behaviour illustrates this rather well. He described how digital shopping environments can introduce consumers to brands and products they were not actively searching for. A person looking for one kind of product may encounter several alternatives and discover something they had not previously considered. The product's presentation, the context in which it appears and the opportunity to explore all contribute to that discovery.

What interests me is not the particular shopping platform or category. It is the underlying principle: discovery can happen before a customer knows exactly what they are looking for.

That is a much richer idea than simply buying more advertising.

A business should not only ask how many people it can reach. It should ask whether it can become relevant to people who have a need, even when they do not yet know the business or the solution by name.

But being found does not mean being understood.

### Recognition is a different problem from reach

Suppose a customer discovers your business. They visit your website, see your presentation or receive your name through a referral.

What happens next?

They try to determine whether you are relevant to them.

This is where **Identifiability** enters the picture.

A customer may understand that you are a technology company, a consulting firm, a healthcare provider or a manufacturer. That does not mean they recognise you as the right answer to their particular situation.

There is a subtle but important difference between knowing what a business does and recognising why that business matters to you.

Consider two statements:

"We provide digital transformation services."

"We help organisations recover control of technology platforms that have become difficult to scale, operate or evolve."

Both may describe the same underlying expertise. But the second gives a particular customer something to recognise. It describes a situation they may already be experiencing.

Identifiability is not about narrowing a business so aggressively that it excludes every other opportunity. It is about making its relevance recognisable.

This is also where generic positioning becomes expensive. When every business describes itself using the same vocabulary, customers have little reason to distinguish one from another.

The business believes it has communicated its capabilities. The customer sees another provider making familiar claims.

The difference between those two perceptions can be substantial.

### Being believed requires more than being seen

Now suppose the customer recognises the relevance. They see a possible fit. The next question is whether they believe the business can deliver.

This is what I mean by **Visibility** in the Six Degrees of Desire.

I know the word is commonly used to describe exposure and reach. That is not how I am using it here. In this context, visibility is about the evidence that makes a business credible in the eyes of a potential customer.

A company can claim twenty years of experience. A consultant can describe themselves as an expert. A product can promise to transform an industry.

But what can the customer actually see that supports those claims?

The evidence may come from previous work, customer experiences, references, reputation, demonstrated expertise, leadership credibility or the way a business responds to difficult questions.

It need not always be a collection of awards and logos. In fact, sometimes a thoughtful explanation of a difficult problem reveals more about an organisation's capability than a page full of credentials.

The point is that credibility must become visible to someone other than the business making the claim.

There is a reason customers ask for references, examine case studies, speak to existing users and look for independent opinions. They are trying to reduce uncertainty.

A business may have the capability to solve a problem, but until the customer has sufficient reason to believe it, that capability remains largely an internal fact.

### The cost of making customers interpret your value

There is another situation I have encountered repeatedly, particularly in technology and professional services.

A business has the right expertise. Its relevance is clear. It has a credible track record. Yet the customer still struggles to understand what is actually being offered.

This is a problem of **Decipherability**.

Businesses often communicate from the inside out. They explain their architecture, methodologies, tools, features, delivery processes and technical capabilities. They assume that because these things are important to the people building the solution, they will be equally meaningful to the people buying it.

They may not be.

A customer buying a technology solution may not care whether a system uses a particular architecture unless they understand what that architecture changes for them. A hospital administrator may not be interested in the technical details of an integration layer, but may care deeply about whether patient information becomes more accessible across departments.

The expertise is important. The communication must translate it.

This does not mean reducing complex work to simplistic promises. It means being able to explain complexity without making the customer do all the interpretive work.

When that translation fails, the customer often resorts to comparing whatever is easiest to compare: price, features, delivery timelines and the number of items included in a proposal.

The business may conclude that the customer does not appreciate its expertise.

Perhaps the customer simply could not decipher its value.

### The price is often where an earlier failure becomes visible

Eventually, the conversation reaches money.

And this is where businesses frequently make another mistake.

They assume that if a customer does not proceed, the price must be too high.

Sometimes it is. But affordability is not quite as straightforward as the number on a quotation.

I call this degree **Affordability**.

A customer may have enough money and still not consider an investment worthwhile. Another may have a limited budget but be prepared to make a significant commitment because the consequences of not acting are greater.

The calculation involves more than purchasing power. It includes perceived value, urgency, risk, alternatives, opportunity cost and confidence in the outcome.

Think about a business that is losing money because of an inefficient process. A proposed solution may appear expensive when considered as an isolated purchase. But when compared with the continuing cost of the problem, the investment may be entirely reasonable.

The reverse is also true. A relatively inexpensive solution can feel like a poor investment if the customer cannot see a meaningful outcome.

This is why discounting is such an unreliable response to every price objection. A discount can make a purchase cheaper. It cannot automatically make it more valuable.

If the customer does not understand what they are buying, has doubts about whether it will work or cannot connect the investment to a meaningful outcome, reducing the price may only postpone the real objection.

### Wanting to buy and being able to buy are not the same

There is one final distinction.

A customer can discover a business, recognise its relevance, believe its claims, understand its offering and accept the investment. Yet the transaction may still not happen.

The proposal may be waiting for approval. The contract may be unclear. The purchasing process may be cumbersome. The decision-maker may not have the authority to proceed. The next steps may be uncertain.

The customer may be willing, but the business has not made it easy to move forward.

This is **Decisionability**.

I find this particularly interesting because businesses often interpret delays as a lack of interest. Sales teams chase the customer, offer another meeting or revise the proposal, when the actual difficulty may be a process that has made the decision unnecessarily difficult to complete.

A decision is not finished simply because someone says they are interested.

The customer must be able to act on that intention.

And the business must make that possible without introducing unnecessary uncertainty at the last moment.

### Six degrees, one connected experience

These six degrees are not six departments, six marketing campaigns or six boxes to tick.

They are connected conditions in the customer's movement towards a decision: **Discoverability, Identifiability, Visibility, Decipherability, Affordability and Decisionability**.

Their order matters, but the experience is not always linear. A customer may revisit an earlier concern after learning something new. A pricing discussion may reveal that the value was never properly understood. A credibility concern may emerge only when a proposal reaches the decision-maker. A customer who was ready to proceed may reconsider when the purchasing process introduces friction.

This is why I hesitate to describe the framework as a conventional funnel. A funnel tends to make us focus on how many people move from one stage to the next. The Six Degrees of Desire asks us to look more carefully at what enables that movement in the first place.

The distinction is important.

If a business has poor discoverability, increasing its conversion rate among existing visitors may not address the larger opportunity. If it has strong discoverability but weak identifiability, more traffic may simply produce more irrelevant enquiries. If customers recognise the business but do not believe its claims, another advertising campaign is unlikely to solve the credibility problem.

The same logic applies further along the journey.

What looks like a pricing problem may be a failure to explain value. What looks like indecision may be a lack of evidence. What looks like a sales problem may be a purchasing process that makes it difficult to say yes.

The symptom is visible. The cause may be several steps away.

And that is where the growth illusion begins: businesses keep improving the things they can easily measure, while overlooking the conditions that actually influence customer decisions.

### Perhaps growth is not about being seen more

I keep coming back to the visiting card.

Jeet suggested changing two words. I have no particular reason to do so. The card does what I expect it to do. It creates curiosity, gives someone a reason to ask a question and starts a conversation.

It does not try to complete the entire relationship in one interaction.

Perhaps businesses could learn something from that distinction.

We have become very good at broadcasting what we do. We have become comfortable measuring how many people see us. We have invested in making our messages louder, our websites more elaborate and our campaigns more frequent.

But attention is only an opportunity. It is not recognition, trust, understanding or a decision.

A customer does not choose a business merely because they have seen it. They choose when enough things come together for that choice to make sense.

That is the thought behind the Six Degrees of Desire.

And perhaps the question businesses should spend more time asking is not, "How do we get more people to see us?"

It is this: **what is preventing the people who need us from choosing us?**

The answer may have very little to do with visibility.

## Frequently Asked Architectural Questions (FAQs)

### Q: What are the Six Degrees of Desire?

Discoverability, Identifiability, Visibility, Decipherability, Affordability and Decisionability: six connected conditions a customer moves through on the way to a decision. Each one is a different reason a willing customer can still fail to buy, and strengthening one does not compensate for a gap in another.

### Q: Why can a highly visible business still struggle to win customers?

Visibility only creates attention, not recognition, trust, understanding or an easy path to decide. A business can be seen everywhere and still fail at identifiability (being recognised as relevant), decipherability (being understood), or decisionability (making it easy to actually say yes), which is where the growth illusion sets in.
