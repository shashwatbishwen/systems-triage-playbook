# Speaking Topic: Speaking Topic: Where FinOps and Technical Due Diligence Overlap

> **Keynote Syllabus:** Executive & Board Briefing | **Speaker:** [Dr. Shashwat Bishwen](https://shashwat.si) | **Canonical Link:** [https://shashwat.si/speaking-topics/finops-and-technical-due-diligence-overlap/](https://shashwat.si/speaking-topics/finops-and-technical-due-diligence-overlap/)

## Keynote Overview
Cost structure is a diligence finding, not a separate spreadsheet handed over after the technical review is done.

## Core Keynote Thesis & Field Notes

Most diligence processes run technical review and cost review as two separate workstreams, often by two separate teams who never compare notes. I argue they are the same finding, viewed from two angles. A platform spending 22 to 28 percent of its cloud budget on observability is not just a FinOps inefficiency, it is a signal about engineering discipline that belongs in the technical risk section.

When I run diligence this way, a cost anomaly is treated as a lead, not a footnote. If a target is over-provisioned for its current scale, that usually traces back to the same undocumented assumptions that create key-person dependency and fragile failover paths.

Separating these workstreams means an acquirer gets two incomplete pictures instead of one accurate one.

## Key Takeaways for Founders, CEOs & Boards

- A cost anomaly in diligence is a technical risk signal, not a separate line item.
- Over-provisioning usually traces back to the same undocumented assumptions that create operational risk elsewhere.
- Running cost and technical diligence as one workstream produces one accurate picture instead of two incomplete ones.

## Executive Audience Q&A

### Q: Isn't cost just a finance question, separate from engineering risk?

Not in practice. The same undocumented assumptions that drive over-provisioning tend to also drive fragile failover paths and key-person dependency.

### Q: How do you convince a deal team to merge these workstreams?

By showing one concrete case where a cost anomaly, followed to its root cause, surfaced a technical risk the engineering review had missed entirely.

## Keynote FAQs

### Q: Should cost review and technical review be run by the same team in diligence?

They should at minimum be run in close coordination, because cost anomalies and technical risk findings frequently share the same root cause.
